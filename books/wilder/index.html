Delivered by Wilder

Who falls for a woman who’s eight months pregnant?
Answer: Sands Wilder. Immediately.

I barely learn his first name before he’s shielding me from bullets.

I’d come to the bank to rescue my confused grandmother, the woman who rescued me when I found myself pregnant after stupidly swiping right, curious about intimacy. Oops.

Only I find a lot more than grandma mentally visiting the 1970s. 

There’s a new security guard in Wicked. A new talking parrot in the bank lobby. And contractions that can’t be labor. Absolutely, positively cannot be labor. Because I’m still weeks away from my due date, right? 

Then three gunmen storm the bank.

ACK!

Seems I’ve acquired my own personal security guard, an undercover ex-Marine with protective instincts bigger than Texas. Because he’s leaning over me, making me laugh even when it hurts, whispering promises that can’t be true. Not after we just met.

“Don’t care,” he tells me, and there’s more than baby feet kicking around my belly at the heat in his eyes. “You’re mine now. You and those babies. Mine to protect. Mine to take care of.”

So now I’m praying we make it out of this bank alive. All of us: me, my unborn twins, my grandma, and the undercover ex-Marine who just promised me forever in the middle of a heist.

I really hope he means it.

EXCERPT

Chapter 1 - WILDER

“Ma’am, you have a wonderful afternoon.” The words come out the same way they have several hundred times since Monday morning.

The middle-aged woman I’m holding the door for gives me a smile and passes through the vestibule, opening the second set of doors on her own and heading for the parking lot. I let the interior door ease shut and resume my station overlooking the lobby.

Day five of bank security detail, and I’m officially bored out of my skull.

This is what you signed up for, Wilder.

Not exactly. What I signed up for was protecting this bank—this town—from a crew of Desert Jackals prospects using small-town robberies as their ticket into the club. Intel said Wicked’s Texas Century branch was next. 

In truth, I volunteered for this particular post. Practically arm-wrestled Breaker for the privilege of stepping in while our regular guy, Jimmy, took his family up to Ruidoso for the week, when we all but insisted he get the heck out of Dodge.

Jimmy might be qualified for what he does, as are all our guys, but unlike most of the men on the Wilder Security payroll, he’s a family man from the word go, not one of the Mountain Kings. Not ex-military. If the Jackals are gonna hit, better to have someone here who’s prepared for a fistfight. A firefight. An all-out war, if the Jackals don’t back off from our turf.

Except nothing’s happened.

The excitement this week equaling my nondescript pewter-gray uniform.

Standing at parade rest all day is second nature after ten years of service. And the uniform itself fits well enough. The shirt’s got the red-and-white Wilder Security patch stitched above the breast pocket (Mom’s doing). My name’s embroidered above it in cursive: Wilder. Not Sands. Just Wilder. The way I prefer it. (Her doing as well.)

I run a hand over my jaw, feeling the scruff I didn’t bother shaving this morning. Overslept. Not my fault, not after the late night Odds and I pulled off. Half drunk on Lone Star and full drunk on bad ideas.

A loud squawk grabs my attention from across the lobby, a slight smile curling one side of my mouth.

“Your tits look great in that top,” the bird announces to one and all.

Maybe the idea wasn’t so bad after all.

Welcome to undercover operations, Wicked, Texas, style. Less high-stakes thriller, more paid opportunity to perfect the art of the polite nod.

At least Harry’s providing entertainment today, a substantial improvement from earlier in the week.

I pull my gaze off the African grey parrot and put it back where it belongs: the entrance.

Wrench and Rambler are in the parking lot, taking over for Breaker who headlined the morning shift. Cutlass is currently on his third loan application of the week—third—trying to charm the cat-eye glasses right off the loan officer’s face. Cutlass refusing to rotate out like the rest of the guys we’ve had coming and going all week, claiming his spot at Z. Simpkins’ desk for a third straight day. Odds and I betting on not how, but how soon those two are going to end up together.

Speak of the devil, the heavy exterior doors explode open, and not from an 80-mile-an-hour gust of wind which is one reason for the double set. It’s pretty calm outside. Still and hot. Nope, the exaggerated swing’s thanks to my kid brother, Ocean, a.k.a. Odds.

Typically, it’s the ever-present deck of cards one notices first. That or his gregarious, loud personality. Today, it’s the shirt that screams LOOK AT ME! The one he threatened Wrench with last night. 

It wouldn’t stand out on a beach. But here? An old-school, glamorous bank in West Texas? Cowboys riding T-Rexes amid tropical hibiscus draws attention, and not the good kind.

No, it’s the kind where I’m squinting and wishing I had sunglasses.

Arm outstretched, he pushes through the interior door I don’t move to open. “Fucker,” he whispers, locking eyes with me and shuffling the deck of cards with rapid-fire snaps. He gives me a subtle wink, a not-subtle nod of his head as he goes straight to the short line in front of the teller windows, flapping both elbows like a bird.

The private joke garners another smile. 

One I attempt to wipe free the second I notice the frowning countenance of Ms. Whitt, the bank vice president who’s in charge this week, aimed his way. She’s none too happy with the “shady sorts who keep loitering in her bank,” as I’ve heard her complain more than once since the Mountain Kings amped up surveillance on Wednesday. She’s got no reason to suspect I ride with them. And I haven’t seen any reason to tell her.

The no-nonsense lady probably has a decade on my thirty-two; she’s been taking her responsibilities seriously, even if she has seen fit to relax long enough to pass on several compliments from long-time Texas Century Bank customers, who’ve been vocal about appreciating my “chivalrous manners.” Mom’ll get a kick out of hearing that if I can find a moment to tell her when no one else is around. Don’t need to ruin my street cred with my siblings.

Boredom, along with thoughts of Mom and Whitt and every other female I might’ve met in the last two decades, blasts to smithereens when I notice the one approaching the exterior doors from the parking lot.

Without conscious decision-making, I’m sprinting past the lobby doors and into the vestibule, to get it for her.

Her, a young woman wearing a flowy, floral top that crisscrosses her chest and ties over the most enormous baby bump I’ve seen outside of a hospital waiting room (and tied beneath breasts I can’t help but notice either).

When did you become a lecher? Checking out a pregnant woman?

She’s got this runaway mass of wavy hair that can’t decide if it’s brown or blonde, streaked through with gold and tumbling over her shoulders like it air-dried this morning and hasn’t been chastised since. One hand’s braced against her lower back, the other clutching her purse strap, and she’s breathless, as though she ran here. 

You’ve been standing in this lobby for four and a half days. You’d find a potted cactus compelling at this point.

I straighten. Already straight—always straight; that’s the Corps for you—but I straighten.

“Ma’am.” The second she’s through the first set of doors, I race past her to open the ones into the bank itself.

“Thank you.” Her gaze sweeps the lobby—a flash of worry in her eyes—before landing on the benches opposite the teller windows, and she just sort of deflates.

I hear her inhale, watch her roll her shoulders up and back, and she slides on her flat-soled shoes to face me.

“Thanks for getting the doors.” She takes another audible breath, and I swear I can practically see her trying to stand down from some place of panic right before my eyes. Her hand rubs at the small of her back. She gives a little grimace she tries to hide. “I’m supposed to be rescuing my grandmother, but at this rate, she might have to rescue me.”

“You’re Mrs. Tucker’s granddaughter.” I know because Chrissy, the teller who started earlier this week, and seems to have captured the interest of my youngest brother, skipped over to me about twenty minutes ago, in between customers, quickly pointing out the gray-haired lady dressed like a working rancher’s wife—denim and worn cowboy boots, long-sleeve button-down shirt (despite the heat of summer), along with her cane and gray bobbed hair, old-fashioned purse snug in her lap—and told me not to let her leave. Said she was confused and reinforcements were on their way.

Jimmy had mentioned it to me as well. Seems Gale Tucker, a long-time Wicked resident and Texas Century Bank customer, has been getting more and more confused of late. For some reason, the bank calls to her, even if she isn’t sure what decade we’re in right now.

“That I am.” The young woman gives me a relieved smile, relaxing even more at my observation.

I can’t help but smile back. “Looks like you’re carrying some precious cargo there. I think you’re entitled to a rescue or two.”

She glances down at her belly, which practically takes up its own ZIP code. A light blush dusts her cheeks as she looks back up at me. 

“You’re not Jimmy,” she says, all soft hair, floral fabric, and weary grace.

I’m instantly, inexplicably captivated.

“That I am not. He’s on a well-deserved vacation.”

“So you’re his temporary replacement? I don’t recall him including door-opening duties.” Her avid gaze inspects my uniform, focusing on the embroidered Wilder over the patch, before returning to my face. There’s a teasing light in her eyes now that shoves the weariness aside. “Chivalry? Is that the ‘Wilder Special’?” 

Oh boy.

The Wilder Special. The way she says that? Accompanied by her bright smile? I hope the boys are hungry, because my goose is cooked.

Chapter 2 - WILDER

“It’s a new service we’re offering,” I say, striving for deadpan. “Wilder Security believes in diversifying. Plus, it keeps me from falling asleep on my feet. If I stand in one spot too long, I start counting the longhorns on the wall.”

She laughs, a rich, clear sound that echoes off the high ceiling. Because if there’s one thing locals all know about Texas Century Bank’s Wicked location, it’s the preponderance of longhorns. From the horns mounted along the perimeter of the ceiling, to Roger Emmett himself, stuffed, and standing sentry just a few feet away.

Both cattlemen and University of Texas grads love their longhorns. Lucky me, I’ve got a life-size, taxidermied one not ten feet in front of me, named after a couple of famed Dallas Cowboys over the years. Least the decorating committee left the cow patties out in the pasture and didn’t bring any of those, bronzed or otherwise, into the bank as they did ol’ Roger here.

“Well, I appreciate it,” she tells me sincerely. “The door. Learning that chivalry isn’t dead.” 

That potent gaze of hers roves up and across my shoulders, my recognizable haircut that hasn’t changed despite being home for a while now, down my torso to my work-booted feet. I see the moment clarity strikes. “You’re military.”

“Yes, ma’am. Marines. Ten years. Four years out.”

“My uncle and two cousins were Marines. Can’t miss that stance.” She pauses, and the little pinch around her mouth tells me what’s coming before she says it. “One of them didn’t make it back. So. Yeah. Thank you. For your service.”

The lobby noise keeps going—Harry click-click-clicking his beak, Odds laughing too loud at the teller window, the ordinary sound of a Friday afternoon—but right here, it’s quiet.

“I’m sorry for your loss, ma’am.”

“Thank you.” She tilts her chin up the smallest amount and then she puts her hand on my forearm—casual-like—and her fingers squeeze once. Don’t even think she realizes she’s done it, but it sends a jolt straight through me. “I mean it when I say thank you for your service. For all of it. I know people say it all the time and it starts to sound trite, but— I mean it.”

No ring on that left hand. Can’t help but notice, her fingers still resting against the gray fabric of my sleeve. Could mean nothing—fingers swell, rings get stored in drawers. But I notice. Curiosity rampages.

Doesn’t matter. She’s carrying someone’s baby, Sands Wilder. Means she’s not yours to wonder about.

“It was my honor, ma’am.” My duty. Protecting others. “Runs in the family,” I tell her now.

“You don’t have to keep calling me ma’am,” she says, pulling her hand back as if she just realized she’s still touching me. “Makes me feel like I should be knitting a doily or something.”

“Doily,” I snort, a sudden vision of my no-longer-with-us great-granny’s living room chairs, the tops all draped with the lacy things. “Not exactly a term you hear much these days.”

“Yeah, well, the way you keep ‘ma’am-ing’ me makes me feel like I’m forty, and I’m nowhere close.” 

“You’re not?” I bend my knees, lower my eyes to hers—traveling nearly a foot to do so—then I sweep my gaze side to side as though I’m the dastardly villain on the movie poster for a really bad, over-the-top black-and-white film from last century. “Shhh, now. Best not let that information get out, ma’am.”

Even though my henchman impersonation sucked, she laughs. “Not even close! You’d have to shave nearly twenty years off that accusation, sir,” she fires back, clasping her hands over her heart as though she’s in the same melodrama, acting the damsel in distress with every bit of awful talent I just showed.

“You’re only twenty?” The words explode out of my mouth, along with zero the amount of fun that just came before.

Way to spoil the moment, dipshit. You never ask a lady about her age.

Only instead of taking offense at my blurted astonishment, she abandons the playacting, fiddles with the long purse strap hanging over one shoulder and answers, back to her regular voice. “Not quite that young. Twenty-two in September, if I survive the rest of summer in this West Texas oven.”

“Where’s the husband?” The question slips out before I can stop it. 

“Out of the picture,” she says, tone a bit clipped. A lot wilted. “By choice.”

Her choice? Or his? I can’t help but wonder if she’s still carrying a torch for the idiot. “Whoever he is, he’s a fool of the highest order.”

Her cheeks take on a faint pink hue, and she swallows hard, looking back at my name patch. 

“Wilder,” she reads the cursive embroidery above my pocket out loud. “Is that a description or a name?”

“Both,” I say, the corner of my mouth twitching. “But mostly just the last name on my birth certificate.”

“Last name? Feel like sharing your first?”

I should not be so damn pleased that she asked. I shouldn’t. 

“Sands.” When her forehead furrows, I add, “Mom’s from California.”

That gets a light giggle. “I like it. Sands.” And the way she says that? Gah. It’s as though she just stroked straight up my thigh. “It’s unique.”

“That’s one way to describe it. Dad talked her down from Sandbar.”

She snorts a laugh, chokes on it, then grabs her belly with a groan. I step forward, my hand hovering over her shoulder. “You all right?”

“Sandbar.” Her delighted doubt can’t be missed.

“Really. Scout’s honor.” If she thinks that’s something, wait’ll I introduce her to my siblings.

Wait. What?

“Sandbar.” She makes a pondering hmm, oblivious to my tortured thoughts. 

I’m supposed to be watching for threats. Scanning the entrances and exits. We might have guys out on the perimeter, giving Rambler a heads-up if any Jackal suspects come close, but the goal is to catch them in the act, not scare them off before they can do anything arrest-worthy. Catch them while protecting our town. My infantry squad leader training is screaming at me to do my damn job.

But all I can do is watch her.

“Sandbar,” she says again with another delightful smile. “I may have to consider that.”

My attention zooms to where she’s drumming fingers on her belly. It’s hard not to miss, given how it’s comparable to its own celestial body, ready to grab everything in its orbit. “You got a name for the little one yet?”

“Done some brainstorming, made a few lists; nothing’s stuck.”

“I’ll loan you my mom. She’ll be in hog heaven, brainstorming along with you.”

“I’ll take her,” she says with a smile (a relieved smile?), then immediately adds, “Are you—”

She cuts herself off, slaps an open palm right over her mouth, and it’s killing me. 

What was she going to say? Am I...what? Taken? Available? Completely insane?

Not at all. Absolutely. Affirmative.

“Pretty tits!”

She gives a light yip and whirls toward the noise, the purse hanging down by her hip slamming into my leg when she stumbles. I reach out to brace her. “Heavens. That’s new.”

“That’s Harry,” I tell her, more than a little distracted by the silky feel of her pretty top against my skin.

“Gimme a handful, babe!” The bird hollers out, continuing his one-man show, amusing customers with near obscenities and his patented half-finished limericks.

She turns back to me, laughing, holding on to her belly. “He’s a riot.”

I force myself to release my hold on her shoulder, curling my fingers in on themselves at the lingering feel of her beneath my palm. “Yep, that he is. The most entertaining thing in the lobby all week which isn’t exactly a compliment to the rest of us.”

“At least now I know what Chrissy meant when she texted about a surprise.”

You’re the surprise, I want to tell her, but instead I go with: “You got a name I can use, or should I keep calling you ma’am before this child gets here?” 

“Ashlinn, with an I and two Ns.” She actually blushes. “Which is stupid. You don’t need to know how to spell it. Ack.”

Oh, but I do.

“Ashlinn,” I repeat, the taste of her name lingering like a shot of prime whiskey. “Pretty. You live local?”

Please say you’re not just visiting. Please, please.

“Moved in with my grandmother a few months ago.” She nods toward the row of cowhide benches across the lobby, glossy marble wall at their back. 

“And has that been for her to help you? Or for you to help her?” I trod right over personal boundaries, asking that, but can’t help myself. I’ve got to know more about mom-to-be Ashlinn.

“She’s been fantastic,” Ashlinn assures me, then ruins it with a sigh, worry clouding her eyes. “When she remembers what year we’re in. Ah!”

She gives a light cry and cringes. Instantly, I’m doing a 360, checking for danger. For anything I missed while my attention hasn’t been anywhere close to my job.

“Sorry,” she gasps, then forces a brief smile. “My back’s been killing me today. Might have overdid it cleaning.”

Your back’s been killing you because you’re about to drop that baby any second now.

I step around her to open the heavy glass door for an exiting couple, giving them a polite nod. 

“Chatting with you proved a highlight I needed today, thank you,” she tells me the moment the door closes behind them. “I’m going to check in with Grandma. See if I can get her home without forceful wrangling.”

“You need a hand, just ask. Mean it.”

And the way she flashes those tired eyes and bright smile my way, tells me my sincerity’s reached through.

* * *

The next few minutes prove an exercise in torture. And I made it through The Crucible at the end of boot camp, so that’s saying something.

Because she’s not taken. Not claimed nor committed.

But she’s preggers! Instant family, Sands.

Yeah. Wouldn’t that be something?

But even I’m not fool enough to be thinking forever after an eight-minute conversation.

You aren’t? So your focus is where it should be? Bank threats?

Uh. No.

Because I’m fixated on her. On her slow-moving progress first over to Chrissy’s teller window after Odds backs off. (I hope Ashlinn isn’t prone to morning sickness. Because the shirt’s enough to induce nausea—no pregnancy needed.)  Then she ambles over to her grandmother, Gale Tucker. Then on to Harry’s domain.

Eh. Eh.

Okay, I admit it. My concentration’s shot.

Vision’s consumed with her.

Thoughts—concern, more like, each time she frowns, grabs at her back—taking a one-way trip to Crazy Town.

Especially when Harry says something and she glances over her shoulder, catching my eye. Her smile hits me like a flashbang, momentarily scrambling all systems. I’m struck by the urge to wrap my arms around her, pregnant belly and all, and usher her to the nearest chair. Offer her water. A pillow. My soul wrapped in gift paper.

What the living hell, Wilder? Get a grip.

Then my eldest brother (yeah, there’re a lot of us Wilders in Wicked, and in the Mountain Kings MC), Wrench, storms in—wearing a scowl and his cut (so much for our incognito presence) demanding a word with Ms. Whitt, bank VP. Whoa boy.

Least the little red-haired thing that slips in moments later brings aromatic goodies with her, in the form of several bright pink Gimme Some Sugar bakery boxes, and a smile to my woman’s—

Er, to Ashlinn’s face, when the two ladies strike up a conversation near Harry.

Wrench and Whitt arguing.

Cutlass charming the loan officer (and after swearing off women the last few years, too).

Odds’s flirting.

And me? Whipped into someone I don’t recognize with a tired if bright smile and a few heartfelt words exchanged over families.

And here I thought surveillance Wicked, Texas–style was boring?

I am so fucked.

Chapter 3 - ASHLINN

I swear I can feel the touch of his gaze on my ass. 

Heating me up better than a long, bubbly soak in the tub.

Not nearly as relaxing, though.

Ugh. Don’t I have enough on my plate? Why waste thoughts on the sexy security guard, wondering if he’s watching me walk across the bank?

Walk? You’re waddling so much these days I’m surprised you haven’t started quacking. Cluck.

Yeah, like that’s going to give me confidence. I squeeze my toes in my ballet flats, hoping I’ve got decent grip across the glossy floor and—after a quick glance shows Grandma content as could be (and, sadly, no obvious recognition when her attention skitters my way)—head over to the teller windows.

Is he watching? Am I a fool for wondering?

I ought to be figuring out how soon I can get Grandma in the car, settled at home, and call my obstetrician about the nagging ache banded across my lower back that’s been stealing my attention since I woke up.

I do not need to be noticing anything about anyone. I don’t!

But—

Boy howdy.

Chrissy’s text had nudged me from an afternoon doze, startling me full awake right quick: ur grandma is here!! making a deposit and calling her ID a prank?? I’m keeping watch don’t panic also WAIT UNTIL YOU SEE WHAT’S DOING STAND UP IN THE LOBBY

Stand-up. As in a comedy routine?

Chrissy certainly hadn’t mentioned the new security guard who holds doors open with a smile that zapped across my middle like a static shock on a dry winter day, and who says yes, ma’am as though he’d been raised in a different century. A tall security guard with gorgeous posture, glittering eyes, and sandy-brown hair, kept military short.

Sandy! My inner self snorts.

Sandbar. Sandbar Wilder.

No, Sands Wilder.

Stop it, Ashlinn. You don’t get to think about hot security guards. You had your chance at romance and blew it on a dating app and a guy named Jason who ghosted you the second you told him you were pregnant.

Ahh, but what if I’d met Sands Wilder instead?

Not doing this. Not today.

A man in a most excellent Hawaiian shirt shuffles cards on the counter in front of Chrissy. She’s laughing, shaking her head at whatever he just said.

The second she spots me, she abandons all pretense of professional composure. 

“Ash!” Her face lights up and she makes a shooing motion at Hawaiian Shirt Man. “Sorry, I’ve got— She’s— Give us one second—”

He turns, takes in the entirety of my eight-months-pregnant self, and steps aside, gesturing me forward with a grin that suggests he’s incapable of being offended. “She’s all yours, darlin’.”

It’s her first solo day at the teller window, and she looks adorably competent in her casual Friday jeans, charm bracelet clinking against the counter. Her brown curls are doing their thing—all of them, concurrently, and in several directions—and her smile’s got the wattage of someone who’s had at least four cups of coffee and has zero regrets about it.

Ah, coffee. I do miss coffee.

“First things first,” she says the moment I reach the wood counter separating us, the decades-old black iron scrollwork going up the sides and over our heads. “Your grandma is fine. She’s just…well—”

“Out to lunch?” I say, which has become something of a commiserating, comforting joke between the two of us. Because the first time Grandma slipped out of the house while I was napping (a little over five weeks ago) and stationed herself at Cuppa Jolene, the local coffee shop, and Chrissy and I split up, tracking her down from two directions, Chrissy had been the one to stumble upon Grandma first. When questioned, Gale Tucker had simply responded that she was “out to lunch” and why in the world was Erica taking an interest in her eating habits?

“She thought her driver’s license was a prank,” Chrissy shares, then bites her lips as though it’s the last thing she wants to tell me. “Swore—and I quote—I do not look anything at all like this horrid picture.”

I close my eyes, inhale down to my swollen feet before opening them again. “What year does she think it is?”

“Seventy-three. Give or take.  She’s okay, Ash, really. And I’ll come over after I get off, spell you a while so you can rest. You saw her, right?” Chrissy aims a pretty red fingernail over my shoulder. “She’s sitting on the bench with her purse and cane, just people-watching.” Chrissy’s expression goes sympathetic-empathetic for a moment, before snapping back to cheerful. “I’m back to being Erica today. She wanted to know if I’d scared off any bears lately.”

Not a good day, then. More All My Children reruns playing inside Grandma’s mind. Erica Kane was always her favorite.

“I know, I just— The days are getting harder, Chrissy. She was great yesterday. Full-on Grandma Gale, made me biscuits, even blasted through the ironing while we watched some AMC reruns.” Yes, Grandma still has some old, viewable VHS tapes of the classic soap she recorded back in the ’90s. “Then this morning she frowned like she couldn’t place me at all.” I shift my weight, and the ache in my back shifts right along with me. “Two good days a week, if I’m lucky. That’s what I’m getting now. Two.”

I’d always loved my childhood summers in Wicked, away from suburban Houston’s heat and humidity. The Wild Rose Mountains felt like freedom, Grandma’s house like a second home.

When I first moved in with her six and a half months ago, fleeing Richmond and my inconvenient timing and my parents’ disappointment, even though they tried to hide it (their understandable embarrassment over my growing “situation”), Grandma had been sharp as ever. A little forgetful, sure, but nothing alarming. One questionable day a week, tops.

Now? I’m lucky if she remembers who I am at both breakfast and bedtime.

The decline’s been steep and fast. Super scary.

Mom and I already videoing about what might have to happen, and when. And—ugh—it’s difficult to even think about me not being able to care for Grandma once my little ones arrive.

Yep, plural.

Not something I’ve told anyone besides Grandma, who promptly forgot. And that was on one of her good days.

“Hey now.” Chrissy yanks me back to the present. “Don’t borrow trouble. Happy thoughts, happy baby.”

She’s right. I know exactly what my dad would say on the topic. He’d quote some variation of Matthew 6:34, about not worrying about the future, letting tomorrow take care of itself.
